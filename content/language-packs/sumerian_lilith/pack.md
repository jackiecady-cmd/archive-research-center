## Dialect / variant notes
- **Dialect or register:** Layer 1 uses literary Sumerian (Old Babylonian
  copies of earlier narrative; first-millennium bilingual incantation
  Sumerian with Standard Babylonian Akkadian). Layer 2 uses Biblical
  Hebrew and Jewish Babylonian Aramaic. Layers 3–4 are medieval Hebrew /
  literary Aramaic and modern English reception. These are not one dialect
  and not one “Lilith language.”
- **Manuscript variants:** The ḫalub-tree passage is cited from the CDLI
  composite Q000364 / P469670. Individual tablets can differ in a sign or
  in line order; this draft does not collate every witness. Thompson 1903
  is an older edition of Utukkū Lemnūtu; Geller 2016 is the modern
  standard and is cited only. The King List line *lil₂-la₂* is
  itself a site of disagreement.
- **Script or orthography notes:** Sumerian is given in typographic
  transliteration from CDLI sign readings (sz → š, {gesz} → ĝeš, numeric
  subscripts as ₂ ₃ ₄, ḫ for the *h* series where that is the usual
  value). This is ORACC-style scholarly transliteration (`languages.md`),
  not a phonetic reconstruction of everyday speech. Hebrew uses standard
  pointed Masoretic spelling; SBL academic transliteration is used beside
  it (î, ê, ā, š).
- **Witness date / distance from original:** Sumerian narrative on Old
  Babylonian tablets; Utukkū Lemnūtu as a first-millennium series;
  Isaiah in the medieval Masoretic tradition of an Iron Age book; Bavli
  and bowls in late antique / early medieval witnesses; the Alphabet of
  Ben Sira and the Zohar in medieval and later copies. None of these is
  a photograph of an “original Lilith autograph,” and they do not form
  a single chain of copies of one work.

## Uncertainties
> ⚠️ **UNCERTAIN** — whether the Sumerian *ki-sikil lil₂-la₂-ke₄* in
> *Gilgamesh, Enkidu and the Netherworld* is “Lilith.” Why: the phrase
> means, literally, “the young woman of the *líl*.” Kramer 1938 treated
> her as Lilith; that equation is a modern scholarly claim, still
> debated, and Kramer’s wording is REFERENCE-ONLY. Readings on the
> table: a líl-spirit maiden in Inana’s tree, not yet the later Jewish
> Lilith; a direct ancestor of Akkadian *lilītu* and later Lilith.
> What would settle it: not a single find. Do not state either side as
> fact. [^1] [^8]

> ⚠️ **UNCERTAIN** — *lil₂-la₂* as the father of Gilgamesh in the
> Sumerian King List. Why: Langdon 1923 read *lil-la* as “fool,
> imbecile, cripple,” and also asked whether it might be a title of
> Dumuzi; others have heard a *lillu*-demon or “phantom.” It is not
> agreed that this line calls Gilgamesh the son of a Lilith-figure.
> [^5]

> ⚠️ **UNCERTAIN** — the folk etymology Lilith < Hebrew *laylâ*
> (“night”). Why: the resemblance is real and already appealed to
> rabbinic readers, but the usual scholarly account derives the name
> from Sumerian *líl* (wind, breath, spirit). Thompson 1903, who is
> PD and is quoted below, already thought a derivation from *laylâ*
> improbable. Likely false as a historical etymology; still part of
> later reception. [^3] [^6] [^14]

> ⚠️ **UNCERTAIN** — identification of the Burney Relief (BM
> 2003,0718.1) as Lilith. Why: the plaque is uninscribed. Suggestions
> have included Lilith, Inana/Ishtar, and Ereshkigal. None is proven.
> The image is LINK-ONLY. Do not caption the plaque “Lilith” as fact.
> [^11]

> ⚠️ **UNCERTAIN** — what Isaiah’s *lîlît* is, in its own poem. Why:
> a waste-dwelling being in a ruin oracle, later read as the demon
> Lilith, and in the KJV as “screech owl.” The word is probably
> related to Akkadian *lilītu*, but Isaiah 34 is not a Sumerian myth
> and does not tell the Ben Sira story. [^12]

## Ready-to-use summary (Archivist / content bots)
- **One-line takeaway:** In Sumerian narrative, a *ki-sikil lil₂-la₂* — a “young woman of the *líl*” — nests in Inana’s ḫalub-tree and is driven out to the waste places; Akkadian incantations name a related class, *lilû / lilītu / ardat-lilî*, as spirits to be expelled, not worshipped. Later Jewish Lilith, the medieval “first wife” story, and modern reclamation are later layers, not the same source.
- **Safe to quote:** Thompson 1903 (PD), e.g. the expulsion lines, “Thou shalt have no food to eat, Thou shalt have no water to drink,” *Utukki Limnuti* Tablet IV, p. 45. Also KJV Isaiah 34:14 (PD), with a note that “screech owl” stands for Hebrew *lîlît*. Lexicon’s readable English may be quoted once Jacqueline accepts this draft; it is not Thompson, ETCSL, or Kramer.
- **Pronunciation guide:** *líl* approximately “leel” (reconstruction, labelled as such). *ki-sikil* “kee-SI-kil.” *lilītu* “li-LEE-too.” Hebrew *lîlît* “lee-LEET.” *Lilith* in English “LIL-ith.”
- **Do not say as fact:** that the ḫalub-tree maiden “is Lilith”; that Gilgamesh’s father was a Lilith-demon; that the Burney Relief is Lilith; that Lilith comes from Hebrew *laylâ* “night”; that Mesopotamian texts prescribe bread, honey, or incense as devotional offerings to Lilītu; that the Alphabet of Ben Sira is a biblical source; that modern practice is evidence for Sumerian cult.
- **Hand-off notes:** Draft for Jacqueline’s review; no READY file; no ritual or worship instruction. Respect practitioners, including those who honor Lilith today, and keep that honor in layer 4. Human-sacrifice disclaimer is in the offerings section and is factual, not a lecture.

### layer: morphologyNotes
- mu 5-am₃ / mu 10-am₃: “it was five years, it was ten years.”
- ĝeš: “tree,” with the wood determinative in the source writing.
- ba-gur₄: “it became thick / massive.”
- ur₂-bi-a: “at its roots.”
- muš tu₆ nu-zu-e: “a snake that does not know incantations” (a snake immune to spells).
- gud₃ … us₂: “set a nest.”
- pa-bi-a: “in its branches.”
- anzu₄{mušen}: the Anzud (Anzû) bird.
- šab-bi-a: “in its middle / trunk” (šab, mid-section of the tree).
- ki-sikil: “young woman, maiden” (ePSD2 s.v. ki-sikil). [^6]
- lil₂-la₂-ke₄: *líl* in the genitive, plus the ergative *-e* (written -ke₄): “the young woman of the *líl*.” *líl*: “wind, breath, spirit” (ePSD2 s.v. lil₂). [^6] ⚠️ UNCERTAIN as an equation with later “Lilith.”

### layer: commentary
This is the earliest extended literary setting in which a *ki-sikil lil₂-la₂* appears: not as a goddess receiving a cult, and not as Adam’s first wife, but as one of three unwelcome inhabitants of a tree Inana had hoped to use for a throne and a bed. The other two are a spell-proof snake and the Anzud bird. Later tradition, and some modern scholarship (Kramer 1938, REFERENCE-ONLY), has called this figure Lilith. That is a possible historical connection and a live debate, not a fact established by the Sumerian wording. ⚠️ UNCERTAIN. [^1] [^7] [^8]

*Ki-el* in Thompson’s older notation is the same word as *ki-sikil*, “young woman.” *Kar-ra* is “fleeing / driven off.”

### layer: morphologyNotes
- lú-líl-la (MULU-LIL-LA): “man / person of the *líl*” = Akkadian *lilû*.
- ki-el-líl-la: “young woman of the *líl*” = Akkadian *lilītu*. Compare the Sumerian *ki-sikil lil₂-la₂* of passages 1–2.
- ki-el-ud-da kar-ra: often equated with *ardat-lilî*, the “maiden of the *lilû*,” a female spirit Thompson describes as more closely involved with human sexual life than *lilītu*. The equation of this third Sumerian phrase with *ardat-lilî* follows Thompson’s bilingual usage; a modern edition may refine the alignment (Geller 2016, REFERENCE-ONLY). ⚠️ UNCERTAIN in fine detail, not in the existence of the triad.
- Thompson already treats *lilû / lilītu / ardat-lilî* as a class of “malignant spirits” in expulsion series, not as cult deities. [^3]

### layer: literalTranslation
The man of the líl, the young woman of the líl, the young woman who flees by (or is driven out in) daylight:
the lilû, the lilītu, the ardat-lilî.

Reading Thompson’s bilingual text. Not Geller.

### layer: readableTranslation
The male líl-spirit, the female líl-spirit, and the líl-maiden: in Akkadian, the lilû, the lilītu, and the ardat-lilî.


### layer: publishedTranslation
Thompson does not always give a facing literary English for every list line. In the introduction to the same volume he names “LILU, LILITU, ARDAT LILI” among “various forms of malignant spirits,” and he uses “Night-wraith” and “Handmaid of the Night-Phantom” for this class. Those are his 1903 English terms, not a line-for-line of the triad above. They are PD and may be quoted as such. His “night” language is a translation choice and does not prove the folk etymology from Hebrew *laylâ*.

### layer: commentary

## The Burney Relief (not a passage)

The so-called Burney Relief, or “Queen of the Night,” British Museum

winged female figure with bird talons, standing on lions, flanked by

## Offerings (informational only)

This section records **what the texts in this pack actually attest**.
It is not a ritual manual, and it is not advice for practice. It is
written with respect for people who honor Lilith today; their practice
belongs in layer 4 and is not evidence for Sumerian or Akkadian cult.

**What layer 1 attests.** The Mesopotamian sources packed here are
almost entirely **apotropaic**: they describe unwelcome presence
(the tree, the sick body, the haunted place) and the driving-out or
binding of that presence. They do not describe a temple cult of
Lilītu, and they do not give a list of foods or incense to be offered
to her in devotion.

- In *Gilgamesh, Enkidu and the Netherworld*, the *ki-sikil lil₂-la₂*
  is expelled from Inana’s tree. No gift is made to her. [^1] [^2]
- In Utukkū Lemnūtu as edited by Thompson, *lilû*, *lilītu*, and
  *ardat-lilî* appear in lists of harmful spirits. Tablet IV, in
  Thompson’s PD English, tells the expelled being: “Thou shalt have
  no food to eat, Thou shalt have no water to drink,” and forbids a
  hand stretched out to Enlil’s table, until the spirit departs from
  the patient’s body. [^4]
- Thompson also notes that Labartu (Lamaštu) “has a whole series of
  texts written against her.” The Lamaštu series, in the modern
  discussion (Farber 2014, REFERENCE-ONLY, not quoted), is the usual
  comparison for **figurines and provisions used to send a demon
  away**. That is expulsion, sometimes with a packet of supplies for
  the road, not worship of Lilītu. It must not be rewritten as a
  Lilītu offering menu, and this pack does not invent bread, honey,
  or incense lists that the Lilītu sources do not support. [^10]

**What layers 2–3 attest.** Late antique and medieval Jewish texts in
this pack are likewise protective, not cultic: Bavli warnings, bowl
amulets “against the Liliths that haunt the house,” and, in the
Alphabet of Ben Sira, amulets that protect infants. Montgomery 1913
describes bowls as a “press” bound on demons, including in the four
corners of a house. These are apotropaic objects and stories, not
offerings *to* Lilith. [^13] [^14] [^15]

**What is not attested in any Lilītu or Lilith source in this pack.**
A canonical Mesopotamian or Jewish ritual of devotional offerings
(bread, honey, incense, or similar) *to* Lilītu or Lilith is not in
the sources reviewed here. If a later pack finds one, it should be
added with a citation. Until then, the honest statement is that we
do not have it.

**Modern practice.** Some contemporary practitioners do offer
incense, wine, candles, or other gifts when they honor Lilith. That
is **modern practice**. It is recorded in layer 4, with respect, and
is not to be read back into Sumerian or Akkadian ritual. [^17]

Human sacrifice is not attested in any Lilītu or Lilith source in
this pack; it was a human practice in some ancient cultures and has
no place in this archive’s material.

# Layer 2 — Talmudic and Jewish late antique (overview)

Isaiah 34:14 is the only occurrence of *lîlît* in the Hebrew Bible:
a being that settles in a ruined landscape, among desert animals and
the *śāʿîr*. The KJV’s “screech owl” is a traditional English
rendering, not what the Hebrew word literally says. A relationship
to Akkadian *lilītu* is widely accepted; identity of sense is
⚠️ UNCERTAIN. The verse does not tell the Ben Sira story. [^12]

In the Babylonian Talmud, Lilith is already a named female demon,
in short notices rather than a long myth:

- **Shabbat 151b:** a man should not sleep alone in a house, lest
  Lilith seize him.
- **Niddah 24b:** a discussion of an aborted fetus that looks “like
  Lilith” (later readers connect this with wings or long hair).
- **Eruvin 100b:** among traits discussed in connection with Eve and
  with women, growing hair “like Lilith.”
- **Bava Batra 73a:** a travel tale mentions Hormin, son of Lilith,
  running on the walls of Mahoza.

English here is a short original summary of the standard pagination,
not a Soncino quotation. [^13]

Late antique incantation bowls from Nippur, in Montgomery 1913 (PD),
treat Liliths as household dangers to be bound or driven out.
Montgomery’s English, of an amulet text in that volume, is:

> This is an amulet against the Liliths that haunt the house of this
> Ephra b. S. and this Bahmanduch b. S. I adjure you, all species of
> Liliths…

The bowls are inverted “presses.” They are not gifts to Lilith.
Object photographs remain LINK-ONLY. [^14]

# Layer 3 — Medieval (overview)

The **Alphabet of Ben Sira** (often dated, with debate, to about the
8th–10th centuries) is the source of the story most people now mean
by “the Lilith story”: God creates Lilith as Adam’s first partner;
she refuses a subordinate sexual position; she leaves; three angels
(Senoy, Sansenoy, and Semangelof, in the usual form of the names)
find her; she is associated with harm to newborns, except where an
amulet bearing those names is used. This is medieval narrative, not
Genesis, not Isaiah, and not the ḫalub-tree episode. It should be
told as a later Jewish story of great afterlife, not as Bronze Age
evidence. A later pack should transcribe a named PD Hebrew printing
(for example Eisenstein, *Ozar Midrashim*, 1915, if that text is the
one used) line by line. This draft only records the outline. [^15]

The **Zohar** and related kabbalistic literature develop Lilith
further, including as a partner of Samael in some passages, and as
part of a wider map of holy and unholy femininity. Modern English
translations are generally still in copyright and are not quoted
here. No practical kabbalistic instruction is offered. [^16]

# Layer 4 — Modern reclamation (overview)

From the late nineteenth century onward, and with particular force
in late twentieth-century feminist writing and in contemporary occult
and Pagan practice, Lilith has been reclaimed as a figure of
independence, refused submission, sexual autonomy, or a “dark
goddess.” Judith Plaskow’s midrash “The Coming of Lilith” is a
widely cited feminist text (in copyright; cite, do not quote at
length). Historical surveys such as Raphael Patai’s *The Hebrew
Goddess* are likewise cite-only.

These readings are **modern perspectives**. They are recorded here
with respect. They are not evidence for what the Sumerian tree
episode, Isaiah 34, or an Akkadian incantation says. They may be
personally meaningful, historically interesting as reception, and
still distinct from layer 1.

Where modern practitioners offer incense, wine, candles, or other
gifts to Lilith, that is **modern practice**. It is not attested as
a Mesopotamian Lilītu cult in the sources of this pack, and it
should not be back-projected onto Thompson or onto Inana’s garden.

This archive does not teach how to worship, evoke, or expel Lilith.
